# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 Background (Cyber Forensics and Digital Evidence)

Cyber Forensics (also called Digital Forensics) is the scientific process of **identifying, acquiring, preserving, analyzing, and presenting digital evidence** in a way that is acceptable for investigative and legal purposes. In modern investigations, evidence can exist in many digital forms—files stored on laptops and mobile devices, logs generated by servers, images captured by CCTV systems, chat messages and emails, cloud backups, browser history, and network traffic traces. Because digital evidence is **fragile** (easy to alter unintentionally) and **high-value** (useful to prove or disprove events), cyber forensic work must follow strict procedures that maintain:

- **Integrity**: the evidence must remain unchanged from acquisition to presentation.
- **Authenticity**: it must be demonstrably the same item that was collected.
- **Confidentiality**: access must be limited to authorized personnel.
- **Availability**: evidence must be retrievable when needed, including months/years later.
- **Accountability**: every action on evidence must be traceable to a user, time, and reason.

A key concept in cyber forensics is the **Chain of Custody**, which is the chronological documentation of how evidence is handled—who collected it, when it was transferred, where it was stored, who accessed it, and what actions were performed. A broken or unreliable chain of custody can reduce trust in evidence and may affect admissibility or credibility during review.

In many real-world setups, evidence is still managed using a combination of manual registers, unstructured folder sharing, and basic storage systems. This can introduce risks such as accidental overwrite, unauthorized access, missing logs, weak identity verification, or incomplete approval controls for court visibility. These risks become more serious when evidence must be retained for long periods, shared across roles, and protected against both external attackers and internal misuse.

## 1.2 Need for Secure Evidence Storage in Cyber Forensics

Digital evidence is different from traditional physical evidence because it can be duplicated perfectly, modified silently, and transmitted instantly. A secure evidence storage platform for cyber forensics must therefore do more than “store files.” It must implement controls that support the **forensic evidence lifecycle**:

1. **Preservation and secure storage** of the collected evidence file and its associated metadata (case reference, description, type, timestamps).
2. **Integrity verification** to prove that stored evidence has not changed (hash values, cryptographic controls).
3. **Controlled access** so that only the correct roles can view, upload, approve, or reject evidence.
4. **Auditability** so that every important event is recorded in a tamper-evident manner.
5. **Court visibility controls** so that evidence becomes court-accessible only after proper review/approval.

Additionally, evidence management systems must be resilient to the evolving threat landscape. Cyber forensic repositories are attractive targets because they can contain:

- case-sensitive documents, images, videos, device dumps, and logs,
- personal identity details (including government IDs and contact data),
- investigation timelines, access histories, and audit trails.

If attackers gain access, they may attempt to steal evidence (confidentiality breach), alter or delete it (integrity/availability breach), or manipulate logs to hide traces of tampering (accountability breach). Therefore, modern evidence storage must combine **strong cryptography**, **robust access control**, and **tamper-evident logging**.

## 1.3 Post-Quantum Security Motivation (Why PQC Matters for Evidence)

Evidence repositories often have **long-term security requirements**. For example, a case may be reopened, appealed, or audited years after the evidence was collected. Traditional public-key cryptography (such as RSA and Elliptic Curve Cryptography) is widely used for secure key exchange and digital signatures today. However, advances in quantum computing are expected to threaten these classical algorithms by enabling efficient attacks (in particular, large-scale quantum computation could weaken RSA/ECC-based security assumptions).

For an evidence system, this creates a “harvest now, decrypt later” risk:

- An adversary may capture encrypted evidence today (or compromise stored ciphertexts),
- and later, when quantum capabilities improve, attempt to recover secrets or forge cryptographic proofs if the system relies only on quantum-vulnerable algorithms.

**Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC)** refers to cryptographic algorithms designed to remain secure even in the presence of quantum adversaries (under current cryptographic assumptions). By integrating PQC into evidence storage—especially for key establishment and signatures—this project aims to improve long-term protection and reduce the risk that stored evidence confidentiality or authenticity could be compromised in the future.

## 1.4 Project Overview: PQC Evidence Storing System

This project, **PQC Evidence Storing System**, is a post-quantum oriented digital evidence management platform designed for cyber forensic use. It provides a structured workflow to store evidence securely, track custody actions, and control court visibility.

At a high level, the system includes:

- A **backend API** that implements authentication, evidence upload/retrieval, approval workflows, and audit logging.
- A **web-based user interface (UI)** for investigators, court users, and administrators.
- An **encrypted evidence store** where uploaded evidence files are stored in encrypted form.
- A **post-quantum cryptography engine** used for key encapsulation and signatures (with symmetric encryption for actual file encryption).
- A **tamper-evident audit mechanism** that records actions as a hash chain, enabling verification and tamper visibility.

### 1.4.1 Primary Roles and Responsibilities

The platform is designed around three operational roles commonly seen in forensic workflows:

- **Administrator (`admin`)**
  - Onboards and manages users (including identity fields and activation status).
  - Views system-level metrics and audit logs.
  - Reviews and approves/rejects evidence for court visibility.
- **Investigator (`investigator`)**
  - Uploads evidence and manages own submissions.
  - Views evidence status and custody history for owned items.
  - Submits evidence for court access after internal review.
- **Court User (`court_user`)**
  - Has read-only access to evidence that is explicitly marked as court-accessible and approved.
  - Uses the system to view evidence and verify its integrity.

### 1.4.2 Core Security Features (High-Level)

The system implements multiple complementary controls:

1. **Role-based access control (RBAC)** to enforce who can perform which actions.
2. **Strong authentication** using access tokens and refresh token rotation to reduce session abuse.
3. **Evidence encryption at rest** so stored files remain protected even if storage is exposed.
4. **Secure key management** where private keys are stored in an encrypted vault outside standard database rows.
5. **Encrypted sensitive database fields** to reduce exposure of identity/case details at the database layer.
6. **Tamper-evident auditing** using a hash chain across audit records.
7. **Approval workflow** that separates evidence submission from court access, supporting procedural control.

These controls are meant to support the key forensic requirements: confidentiality, integrity, traceability, and controlled disclosure.

## 1.5 Problem Context and Motivation

Cyber forensic investigations frequently involve sensitive digital artifacts such as device images, recovered files, logs, and incident records. The value of these artifacts is determined not only by what they contain but also by the confidence that they were:

- collected and stored correctly,
- not altered after collection,
- accessed only by authorized personnel,
- and handled with a defensible chain-of-custody record.

However, evidence management can face common practical challenges:

- **Centralized storage without encryption** can expose evidence if storage is compromised.
- **Weak identity onboarding** can allow impersonation or unauthorized access.
- **Insufficient audit trails** can make it difficult to prove who accessed evidence and when.
- **Non-tamper-evident logs** can be edited by insiders or attackers, weakening trust.
- **No approval gating** may allow court access to evidence before internal validation.
- **Long-term cryptographic risk** may affect evidence confidentiality and authenticity in the future.

The motivation of this project is to provide a structured solution that addresses these challenges using a combination of forensic workflow controls and cryptographic protections, with special emphasis on post-quantum readiness.

## 1.6 Problem Statement (High-Level)

In cyber forensic environments, digital evidence must be securely stored and managed with strong integrity guarantees, controlled access, and an auditable chain of custody. Many existing storage approaches do not provide adequate protection against tampering, unauthorized access, and evolving cryptographic threats.

Therefore, the core problem addressed by this project is:

> To design and implement a digital evidence storage and management system that ensures confidentiality and integrity of evidence, enforces role-based access and approval workflows, and provides tamper-evident auditing with post-quantum-oriented cryptographic mechanisms.

## 1.7 Scope of the Project

The scope defines what the project includes and excludes so that the investigation remains focused and evaluable.

### 1.7.1 In-Scope

The project covers the following functional and security scope:

- **User and role management**
  - Admin-controlled onboarding/registration.
  - Role assignment and activation/deactivation.
  - Support for investigator, court user, and admin accounts.
- **Secure authentication and sessions**
  - Token-based authentication and refresh token rotation.
  - Protection against common session risks (e.g., long-lived tokens).
- **Evidence intake and storage**
  - Uploading evidence files with case metadata.
  - Cryptographic hashing to support later integrity checks.
  - Encryption of evidence files stored on the server.
- **Chain of custody and auditability**
  - Recording actions such as upload, access/view, approval, rejection, and submission for court.
  - Maintaining tamper-evident audit integrity using hash chaining.
- **Approval workflow for court access**
  - Evidence status tracking (pending/approved/rejected).
  - Court access limited to evidence that is approved and marked court-accessible.
- **Post-quantum oriented cryptographic integration**
  - Key encapsulation mechanisms (KEM) for secure symmetric key handling.
  - Digital signatures for metadata integrity/authentication (where applicable).

### 1.7.2 Out-of-Scope

To keep the project bounded, the following are not the primary focus:

- Performing deep forensic analysis (e.g., memory forensics, malware reverse engineering) inside the platform.
- Collecting evidence directly from devices using hardware write blockers or specialized acquisition tools.
- Full legal automation (court e-filing integration, automated compliance generation) beyond controlled evidence access.
- Large-scale distributed storage across multiple regions or full enterprise SIEM integration.

These items may be considered future enhancements, but they are not required to meet the core objectives of secure evidence storage and custody tracking.

## 1.8 Project Objectives

The objectives below describe what the system is intended to achieve. They are written as concrete goals that can be evaluated through implementation and testing.

1. **Develop a secure evidence management platform** suitable for cyber forensic workflows.
2. **Implement role-based access control** for administrators, investigators, and court users.
3. **Provide secure evidence upload and encrypted storage**, ensuring confidentiality at rest.
4. **Enable integrity verification** using cryptographic hashes and verifiable metadata.
5. **Maintain an auditable chain of custody**, recording all key actions with timestamps and user identity.
6. **Ensure tamper-evident audit logging** using a hash-chain approach for audit record integrity.
7. **Introduce post-quantum oriented cryptographic mechanisms** for key handling and signatures to improve long-term security posture.
8. **Support an approval workflow** that ensures evidence becomes court-visible only after authorized review.

## 1.9 Intended Users and Stakeholders

Even though the system is a technical platform, it serves multiple stakeholders:

- **Investigators / forensic analysts** who upload and manage evidence and rely on accurate custody history.
- **Administrative supervisors** who validate user onboarding, monitor system activity, and approve evidence.
- **Court-facing users** who require read-only, controlled access to evidence and confidence in integrity proofs.
- **Auditors / reviewers** who need reliable logs to validate processes and detect tampering attempts.

By designing around these stakeholders, the system aligns technical controls with practical forensic process needs.
